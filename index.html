How these were rebuilt, and all nine at a glance

How to read these. Patient lines marked (real) are the patient's actual words from that chat (some trimmed or merged, said so each time). Lines marked (expected) are built only from facts this patient gave elsewhere in the same real chat; a real patient may answer differently, which is what the next step will find out. No fact is invented. Names are left out: name stands for a real name. "Vera — what I'm thinking" is one extra message that appears once, just before Vera's next question.

Read the "ideal" counts as the best case. Every real line was a real answer to a different question, so the patient might need one more message to say the same thing. The expected lines (one compresses five real answers) are the least proven.

What breaks the "talking to a doctor" feel

#What happensEvidence
1It is a quiz. Over half the questions are yes/no, a third are lists of options inside the sentence, 1 in 20 is open. A third of all patient answers are a plain "no".90 yes/no, 54 lists, 8 open of 172. 56 answers start with "no". Median answer 4 words; 41% are 3 words or fewer.

What already works (keep and do more of)

  • A reaction that carries information. The best turns tell the patient why the next question matters:
    • "That timing after the shawarma does make food upset a real possibility." (b9d47118-1)
    • "Sores in the mouth with drooling and fussiness often point to a viral infection of the mouth." (c0235ec7-1)
    • "Waves of cramping low in the belly fit with a gut infection." (b9d47118-1)

    The model already does a small version of "thinking aloud", even though the prompt forbids it.

  • Hearing a fear and answering it in a few words. "ye dar hona natural hai" (39ae9855-1). "That's okay, a lot of people don't keep track" (0debe088-1). "Thanks for being open about that" (fe2f0f97-1).
  • Mirroring the language. In 39ae9855-1 Vera spoke Hinglish and the patient wrote Hinglish.
  • A question that invites the patient's own words got the longest answer in all 34 chats (49 words): "By 'motion not fresh', do you mean loose watery stools?" (3f64258e-1). Also "Ye gud gudi feeling kab se ho rahi hai?" (17 words, with the cause and a worry).
  • The patient matters as much as the question. The 12 longest answers come from 6 accounts, 4 of them from one. Short answers are partly a style, so the design has to work for both styles.

How we want the patient to feel when it ends

The patient should feelWhat makes that happen
Welcome, no form to fill inAn open invitation first. No list of questions.
Heard, never repeating themselvesUses their own words. Never asks twice. Never asks what the profile already says.
Understood, "she gets what it's like"One accurate line about the experience, not a guess at their mood. If the cue is a fear, answer the fear.
Guided, not interrogatedA short reason now and then ("so I can tell a tummy bug from…"). One "what I'm thinking" message.
Safe to say the awkward thingA normalising half-sentence before a private question. Accept euphemisms. Never demand the exact word.
Unhurried but movingAbout 5 to 6 patient messages for a simple complaint, up to 9 or 10 for a complicated one. Never the same question twice.

The shape of an ideal chat

T1  Patient   first message
    Vera      Vague, or only a greeting  -> an open invitation ("tell me however it comes").
              Rich message               -> straight to the first question.
T2  Patient   answers
    Vera      GAUGE: did they say what it feels like and how it affects them?
              No  -> ONE push question (once only), see section 4.
              Yes -> a reaction in their words + the first narrowing question.
T3  Patient   answers
    Vera      reaction + "when / how did it start?" (a story question).
T4  Patient   answers
    Vera      "WHAT I'M THINKING" (once): a short message saying what this could be so far
              and that it is why Vera asks next, then the next question.
T5+ Patient   answers
    Vera      plain question  /  a guess ("sounds like X, is that close?", at most every other turn).
              Warning signs that go together: ONE "any of these" sentence.
End Vera      check-back: "So far I've heard A, B, C. Is there something else I should know?"
    Vera      assessment.

Every reply is built the same way: a reaction in the patient's own words (at most 10), then, now and then, a reason (at most 10), then one question (at most 12). About 30 words in total. Not every reply needs all three. The first word is not always "Okay".

What "one thing" means. "Is he drinking water or milk?" is one thing. "Does he have a fever, and since when?" and "Have you taken anything for the cold, or flown recently?" are two. The one exception, flagged as decision 1 in section 8: a set of warning signs that belong together (hydration: drinking and passing urine; fever, chills or blood; stroke signs; signs of an infection spreading) can go in one short "any of these" sentence, because the patient answers it with one word ("no"). Unrelated questions are never joined.

Evidence behind the draft's choices

ChoiceBacked byStrength
Open invitation, patients finish quicklyLangewitz 2002, BMJ: 335 patients, mean spontaneous talking time 92 s, median 59 s, 78% finished within 2 minA real study, in person
"Is there something else…?"Heritage 2007, J Gen Intern Med: "something" removed 78% of unmet concerns, "anything" did no better than controlA randomised trial, in person
Acknowledge, prompt, probeXiao et al. 2020: a chatbot got 90 vs 64 words per answer and more disclosure than a plain formGamers and a film trailer, not health

What this means for the prompt (not written yet)

  • One thing to answer, not a word ban. "Food or water" is one thing; "fever, and since when" is two. The "no and/or" rule blocks the first and still lets some of the second through.
  • A reply is a reaction, an occasional reason, and one question. About 30 words. Not always the same opener.
  • Open invitation, then gauge, then one push. Skipped when the first message is already rich. The invitation is the whole reply.
  • "What I'm thinking" once, in the reply to the second or third answer. After that, replies react to what the patient said and do not repeat what it might be.
  • Name a few places in the sentence for pain, swelling or a rash, with "somewhere else" last. For private areas, a plain-words place and a normalising half-sentence.
  • Never say "file". Never say a name the patient did not type. Say "your list" if it has to be mentioned.
  • Never ask the same thing twice. Never re-ask a "not sure".
  • A reply always ends with a question or an invitation (an emergency message is the one exception).
  • The question cap: the invitation turn and the push do not count toward the 8.
  • Vera writes as a woman in Hinglish.

What a first test of such a prompt showed (made-up patients played by a small model, 12 chats per prompt; drafts kept in benchmark/prompt_v2/):

  • Double questions went from 2 to 0 and visible self-corrections from 1 to 0. It opened with an invitation in 7 of 8 short first messages (today's prompt: 0 of 8).
  • It still restated what the problem might be more often than today's prompt (1.6 turns a chat against 0.8), and ran longer (28 words median against 22).
  • Without grouped warning signs, chats were no shorter (7.5 turns against 8.1). The grouped sentence in decision 1 is what gets to 5-6.
  • A speed question was left open: in one round the new prompt's slowest calls ran several seconds longer than today's (90% under 8.8 s against 3.7 s). That could be the rules making the model think longer, or an API slow patch; it was not measured further.
  • The simulated patients are a guess at how people answer. The test shows what the prompt does, not whether real patients open up.

Decisions for the PM

  1. Warning signs: one at a time, or one "any of these" sentence? These drafts use the one sentence ("Any fever, chills or blood in the stool?"), which is how a doctor asks and is what makes 5-6 messages possible. One at a time is closer to the sketch's "no multiple questions in one" but makes the chat longer and more like a quiz. This is the biggest call. The same stretch of conversation D both ways:

    Across a chat the one-at-a-time version adds 2 to 4 messages.

  2. "What I'm thinking" as one extra message, once, just before the next question. Is this the right place and the right length?
  3. The check-back before the assessment ("Is there something else I should know?"). Does it count toward the 8 questions? I suggest no.
  4. Target length. About 5 to 6 patient messages for a simple complaint and 8 to 10 for a complicated one. Is that right?
  5. Typing for someone else. One line, once ("If she's nearby, she can add to it too"). Agreed?
  6. Hinglish in the feminine ("samajh gayi", "rakh rahi hoon") now that Vera has one voice.
  7. The emergency message stays exactly as it is. The only change is the way there.
